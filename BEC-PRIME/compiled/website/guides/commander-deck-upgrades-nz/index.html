How do I know if my Commander deck actually needs upgrades?

Before buying another card, check whether the problem is really power. A deck can have expensive staples and still stumble because its mana, card flow, interaction or win condition does not fit what the deck is trying to do.

Practical guide · 21 September 2026 · These are starting ranges, not rules.

1. Check these five things first

CheckUseful starting pointWhat to ask
Mana baseAbout 34–38 lands for many casual decks, adjusted for curve, ramp and commander.How often do you miss land drops? How many lands enter tapped? Can the deck make every colour it needs?
RampAbout 8–12 dedicated ramp pieces is a useful starting range for many decks.Can the deck consistently move from its early turns into its important 4–6 mana plays?
Card drawAim for roughly 8–12 meaningful sources of repeatable or reliable card advantage.After your opening hand, can the deck keep finding cards without relying on one permanent?
InteractionRoughly 8–12 pieces across removal, sweepers and stack interaction where appropriate.Can you answer a threatening permanent, spell or board state before it runs away?
Win conditionAt least one clear primary route, plus enough redundancy or support to reach it.What actually ends the game, and how many cards have to line up before it can happen?

These ranges deliberately overlap. A low-curve aggressive deck, a five-colour value deck and a seven-mana commander should not be forced into the same spreadsheet.

2. The numbers can be right and the deck can still be wrong

Ratios are diagnostics, not verdicts. A three-mana commander leading an aggressive deck may not need the same mana structure as a seven-mana commander whose entire plan is built around expensive spells. A deck with 38 lands is not automatically healthier than one with 35. The important question is whether the resources match the deck's actual curve and plan.

Look for mismatches such as:

  • Too many tapped lands for a deck that needs to develop quickly.
  • Ramp that does not produce the colours the deck actually needs.
  • Card-draw spells that are powerful but too expensive for the deck's curve.
  • Interaction that answers the wrong kinds of threats for the tables you actually play.
  • A win condition that looks powerful on paper but is difficult to assemble, protect or reach.

3. Expensive cards are not automatically upgrades

One of the easiest traps is upgrading individual cards instead of upgrading the deck. A famous staple can be weaker in your deck than a cheaper card that produces the exact resource your strategy lacks.

Ask three questions before buying:

  1. What job does this card perform?
  2. What card leaves the deck if I add it?
  3. Does the replacement improve the deck's actual plan, or only its reputation?

4. When a score is not enough

Automated deck checkers are useful for finding obvious structural problems. They are less useful when the question becomes contextual: which card should be cut, which weakness matters most at your table, whether a particular engine is worth protecting, or which three changes produce the largest improvement for the money.

A score can tell you that your deck is light on interaction. It cannot, by itself, know which interaction fits your commander, curve, colours, budget and local games. That is where a human review becomes useful.

5. What should you upgrade first?

Fix the bottleneck that causes the deck to fail most often. If you regularly miss land drops, buying another flashy finisher is unlikely to solve the problem. If you empty your hand and cannot recover, adding another combat trick may not help. If the deck reaches its game plan but cannot protect it, the next upgrade may be interaction rather than more threats.

Simple rule: do not ask “what is the strongest card I can afford?” Ask “what is the most important job my deck currently fails to do?”

6. Do I need a full collection to work this out?

No. Start with the 100-card decklist you actually play. Your collection matters later when choosing replacements, but the first diagnostic question is whether the deck's current structure supports its intended game plan.

7. New Zealand buying note

Prices and availability move constantly. Compare the delivered NZD cost, not just the sticker price. A card that looks cheaper overseas can stop being cheaper after shipping, currency conversion and condition differences. For expensive singles, verify the exact printing, language, foil status and condition before comparing listings.
